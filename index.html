      About the Analytics

      How the projections work

      Statistical models built on seven seasons of NFL data, checked against games they had never seen. This page explains what they predict, what goes into them and how well they do, in plain terms.

      What the site predicts

      One number per player per week: the fantasy points he is expected to score in full-PPR scoring, plus a range of likely outcomes.

      Who

      Every team's fantasy-relevant starters

      The starting QB, top two running backs, top three wide receivers, top two tight ends, kicker and defense for each team, from the current depth chart. Anyone ruled out or on injured reserve is removed and the next player moves up.

      What

      Points, a range, odds and a stat line

      The projection is the average outcome. The 80% range, the boom and bust odds and the start/sit odds all come from how far real scores landed from similar projections in past weeks. Box scores break each projection into yards, catches and touchdowns. Projections are available in PPR, half-PPR and standard scoring.

      When

      Updated as news breaks

      Refreshed Thursday at 8 PM, Sunday at 9 AM and Sunday at 12:30 PM Eastern, plus extra runs before holiday and Saturday games, so late injury news is included.

      How a projection is built

      Every refresh runs the same five steps.

      Gather

      Download the latest box scores, play-by-play, snap counts, schedules with betting lines, injury reports, rosters and depth charts.

      Who's playing

      Start from the depth chart, remove players who are out, doubtful or on IR, and move the backups up.

      Describe each player

      Turn each player's recent games and this week's situation into about 50 numbers: usage, red-zone work, matchup, Vegas lines, injuries, weather.

      Predict

      The model, trained on every game since 2020, weighs those numbers and produces expected points.

      Range & publish

      Add the 80% range, save a timestamped copy for grading, and update this site.

      What goes into the numbers

      The model looks at the things that drive fantasy points. Most of them are about opportunity: who gets the ball, and where.

      Usage

      Recent form

      Points, targets, carries, receptions, yards, air yards and share of the team's targets and carries. Recent games count the most.

      Scoring chances

      Red zone & snaps

      Targets and carries inside the 20, carries inside the 5, end-zone targets, and how many plays the player is on the field for.

      Role changes

      Next man up

      When a starter is out, his usual carries and targets are re-shared among the teammates who are left, so a backup is projected for starter's work.

      Matchup

      Opponent

      How many points this defense has allowed to the position compared with an average defense.

      Vegas

      Betting lines

      The point spread and over/under give an implied team score: teams expected to score more produce more fantasy points.

      Availability

      Injuries

      The player's own status and practice participation, plus opportunity left behind by injured teammates.

      Conditions

      Weather

      A small penalty for wind over 15 mph or temperatures below freezing or above 85°F at outdoor stadiums.

      New players

      Rookies

      After two NFL games, a rookie is projected from his own usage. Before that he gets a typical projection for his position and is tagged "new".

      Quarterbacks

      Quarterbacks score mostly by passing, so they have their own model with their own inputs: passing attempts, yards, touchdowns, interceptions, sacks and efficiency, rushing, how fast and how pass-heavy their team plays, the opponent's pass defense, Vegas lines, weather, injuries and receivers who are out.

      It is trained on each team's expected starter in every past game, decided before kickoff, so relief appearances don't muddy it. QB scoring uses 4 points per passing touchdown, 1 point per 25 passing yards and −2 per interception. QBs are not included in the Flex list.

      Picking the models

      Seven kinds of model were built and put through the same test. Each was trained only on games before the week it predicted, then scored on all of 2025 and the first nine weeks of 2026. That is 27 weeks of games no model had seen.

      Running backs, receivers, tight ends

      Average miss in points on 27 unseen weeks. Further left is better.

      Scored on fantasy-relevant players (top 30 RB, 40 WR, 15 TE each week). Avg miss = average difference from the real score, in points. Start/sit = how often it picked the higher scorer from a pair of same-position players.

      Ridge regression is used for running backs, receivers and tight ends, and lasso regression for quarterbacks. Both are linear models: each input gets a weight, and the projection is the weighted total. They tied with or beat the more complex models because week-to-week fantasy scoring is noisy. Flexible models end up memorizing the noise, while ridge and lasso stay steady and their weights can be explained.

      A single model for all three skill positions also beat separate RB, WR and TE models (average miss ). One model learns from more games.

      OLS: classic straight-line regression.
      Ridge / Lasso: regression that shrinks the weights so it doesn't overreact to noise.
      Decision tree: a flowchart of yes/no splits.
      Random forest: an average of hundreds of trees.
      Gradient boosting: trees built one after another, each fixing the last one's mistakes.
      Neural network: layers of weighted sums that can bend to fit curves.

      What moves the projection

      To see which inputs matter, each group was scrambled in turn and the model was re-scored on 2026 Weeks 1-9. The bar is how many points worse the average miss got without that group's information.

      Extra miss when a group is scrambled (RB/WR/TE)

      Recent usage and form carry most of the weight: who has been getting the ball is the best guide to who will get it next. Role changes come second, because they catch backups stepping in. Red-zone work and snap share come third.

      Vegas lines, matchups, injuries and weather barely move the average. That doesn't make them useless. They matter in the few games where they apply, like a backup starting or a 25 mph wind, and those games are a small share of all games.

      Tuning the penalty

      Ridge and lasso add a penalty that keeps the model from chasing noise. Its strength, alpha, was tuned by training on earlier seasons and testing on later ones. Too little penalty and the model overreacts; too much and it ignores real signal.

      Model vs ESPN, week by week

      Each week the model's projections and ESPN's are both saved before kickoff, then scored on the same players once the games are final. Players who did not play are left out of both, and nothing is edited after the fact.

      Ground rules & limits

      No peeking ahead

      Every input for a week uses only information available before kickoff. The backtest follows the same rule, so its accuracy is what you can expect live.

      It's an average, not a ceiling

      Projections sit near the middle of each player's outcomes, so they rarely go above about 22 points. Use the range to judge boom-or-bust potential.

      Late news

      Inactive lists come out 90 minutes before kickoff. A player ruled out after the last refresh may still show a projection.
